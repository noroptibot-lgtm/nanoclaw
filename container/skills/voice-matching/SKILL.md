# Voice Matching Skill

This skill provides the methodology for extracting a client's voice from their content and replicating it authentically across email sequences. Voice consistency is the #1 quality signal for email -- if subscribers feel like the emails were written by a different person than the website, trust collapses.

## Core Principle

Good voice matching is not about copying surface-level style. It's about understanding the underlying thought patterns, emotional tendencies, and communication preferences that make someone sound like themselves.

A voice profile should be specific enough that a writer who has never spoken to the client can produce email sequences that feel unmistakably like them.

## The Voice Analysis Framework

### Dimension 1: Tone Spectrum

Rate the creator on five axes. Use the source content as evidence.

| Dimension | Left | Center | Right |
|-----------|------|--------|-------|
| Formality | Casual slang, abbreviations | Conversational, natural | Polished, precise |
| Energy | Calm, measured, reflective | Engaged, steady | Intense, urgent, passionate |
| Warmth | Clinical, detached | Friendly, approachable | Intimate, personal |
| Authority | Peer-level, exploratory | Confident guide | Definitive expert |
| Humor | Deadpan serious | Occasional wit, dry humor | Playful, comedic |

**How to rate**: Don't average. Find where the creator's default sits, then note when they shift. Example: "Defaults to conversational, shifts to intense when discussing their area of expertise."

### Dimension 2: Vocabulary Fingerprint

Every creator has a vocabulary fingerprint -- words and phrases that are uniquely theirs or that they use with distinctive frequency.

**What to capture**:
- **Signature phrases**: Recurring expressions. "Here's the thing", "Let me break this down", "The math doesn't math"
- **Technical vocabulary**: Jargon they use fluently. Do they say "ROI" or "return on investment"? "Content" or "posts"? "Clients" or "customers"?
- **Casual markers**: Contractions (they're vs. they are), filler words (like, honestly, look), slang
- **Intensifiers**: How they emphasize. "Very"? "Incredibly"? "Insanely"? None at all?
- **Transition words**: "But", "So", "Here's why", "The problem is"
- **Cultural references**: Do they reference pop culture, sports, business, philosophy?

**The Avoid List is as important as the Use List.** If a creator never uses corporate jargon, writing "leverage our synergies" in their voice is an immediate tell.

### Dimension 3: Sentence Architecture

How someone constructs sentences reveals their thinking patterns.

- **Length distribution**: Mostly short (under 15 words)? Mixed? Long and flowing?
- **Opening patterns**: Do they start with "I"? Questions? Bold statements? "So..."?
- **Punctuation style**: Em dashes? Ellipses? Exclamation marks? Minimal punctuation?
- **List style**: Numbered? Bulleted? Inline ("first... second... third")?
- **Paragraph density**: One-liners? 3-4 sentence blocks? Dense paragraphs?

### Dimension 4: Rhetorical Patterns

The persuasion and communication strategies they default to:

- **Questions**: Do they use rhetorical questions? How often?
- **Analogies**: Do they explain concepts through metaphor?
- **Stories**: Do they open with personal anecdotes? Use case studies?
- **Data**: Do they cite numbers? Sources? Or argue from experience?
- **Direct address**: "You" vs. "we" vs. "they" vs. "one"
- **Imperatives**: Do they give direct commands? ("Stop doing X.") Or suggest? ("You might want to...")
- **Contrarian framing**: Do they position ideas against conventional wisdom?

## Email-Specific Voice Adaptation

Voice is not a single thing -- it shifts by context. The same person sounds different on X/Twitter vs. in a newsletter. Email has its own specific characteristics.

### Email Is the Most Intimate Platform

Email sits in someone's personal inbox. It's read in a personal context. This means:
- Email voice should be warmer and more personal than website copy
- Even professional brands can be more conversational in email
- The reader expects to feel like they're being spoken to directly

### The Email Intimacy Spectrum

| Intimacy Level | Characteristics | Suits |
|---------------|----------------|-------|
| Personal letter | Vulnerable, first-person stories, "I" heavy, raw | Individual creators, coaches, solopreneurs |
| Smart friend | Casual expertise, relatable, conversational, occasional humor | Personal brands, small agencies, creators |
| Trusted advisor | Professional warmth, authoritative but approachable | B2B, professional services, consultants |
| Brand broadcast | Consistent branded voice, less personal, still conversational | Larger brands, multiple team members sending |

### Sign-Off Styles

The sign-off reveals a lot about the email's intended intimacy level:
- "Talk soon, [First Name]" -- casual, personal, creator-style
- "Best, [First Name]" -- professional, slightly formal
- "[First Name]" alone -- confident, modern, minimal
- "Warmly, [First Name] [Last Name]" -- warm but formal
- "In your corner, [First Name]" -- community/coaching vibe

Never use "Kind regards" or "Best regards" unless the brand is very formal B2B. These feel cold and corporate for most email contexts.

### P.S. Lines

The P.S. line is often the most-read part of an email after the subject line (reader scanning behavior: subject → last line → P.S. → body).

**When to use P.S.**:
- To add a secondary hook or insight that didn't fit the main email
- To include the offer link a second time (for scanners)
- To add a personality moment that breaks the formal flow
- In sequences where multiple CTAs appear (keep the main CTA once, use P.S. for a link or secondary note)

**P.S. voice**: Often more casual and direct than the email body. Feels like an afterthought that snuck in. This is intentional -- it reads as more authentic.

### How Voice Shifts by Email Type

The same brand voice manifests differently across sequence types:

**Welcome sequence**:
- Warmest, most personal emails in the system
- Excitement about the subscriber joining
- Generous with value (giving away good stuff)
- No sales pressure -- building relationship first

**Nurture sequence**:
- Settles into a "trusted guide" role
- Consistent cadence creates familiarity
- Voice can be slightly more confident/authoritative as trust builds
- Still warm but more substantive

**Re-engagement**:
- Honest and direct -- this is not the time for performative enthusiasm
- A bit more subdued than welcome sequence
- Respectful of their disengagement (never guilting)
- Should feel like a real conversation, not a template

**Promotional / Campaign**:
- Most testing ground for voice -- can feel salesy if not careful
- The key: promotional content is still THIS PERSON selling, not a "campaign voice"
- Their signature phrases should appear in promotional emails too
- Urgency language should match their natural communication style (calm person shouldn't suddenly sound frantic)

**Newsletter**:
- Most "them" of all formats -- it's their regular voice, no specific arc
- Personal opening hook is the place for the most authentic, unfiltered voice
- The tone should feel like catching up with a knowledgeable friend

## Voice Replication Technique

### The 3-Pass Method

When writing in someone else's voice:

**Pass 1: Content First**
Write the substance without worrying about voice. Get the ideas, structure, and information right.

**Pass 2: Voice Overlay**
Rewrite with the voice profile open. Check every sentence against:
- Would they use this word? (vocabulary check)
- Would they phrase it this way? (sentence architecture check)
- Is this their tone? (tone spectrum check)
- Does this sound like them or like a content mill? (authenticity check)

**Pass 3: Email Polish**
Adapt for email conventions: short paragraphs, mobile-first formatting, single CTA, preview text.

### Voice Verification Questions

After writing, ask yourself:
1. If I read this aloud, would it sound like a natural thing this person would say?
2. If I mixed this in with their actual website copy or social posts, could a close reader tell the difference?
3. Am I using their words, or am I using words I think sound like them?
4. Did I capture their energy level, or is this flatter/more hyped than their natural state?
5. Would this creator be embarrassed to have this sent under their name?

## Common Voice Matching Failures

### 1. The "AI Polish" Problem
**Symptom**: Content sounds clean and professional but lacks personality. Missing rough edges, humor, vulnerability.
**Fix**: Add back the creator's casual markers, filler words, and imperfect phrasing. Real people don't write like press releases.

### 2. The "Corporate Drift" Problem
**Symptom**: Voice starts casual and drifts toward corporate language as the sequence gets longer. Email 1 sounds like the creator; Email 9 sounds like a legal department.
**Fix**: Check the second half of every sequence against the voice profile. Drift is most common in emails that involve selling or making serious points -- the instinct is to "get professional."

### 3. The "Surface Copy" Problem
**Symptom**: Using the creator's phrases but missing their thought patterns. The words are right but the logic is wrong.
**Fix**: Focus on HOW they argue, not just what words they use. Match their reasoning style and rhetorical patterns, not just their vocabulary.

### 4. The "Exaggeration" Problem
**Symptom**: Amplifying the creator's personality traits beyond their actual range. Making a slightly funny person sound like a comedian. Making a confident person sound arrogant.
**Fix**: Stay within the ranges identified in the tone spectrum. If they're "occasionally witty," don't write jokes in every paragraph.

### 5. The "Wrong Intimacy Level" Problem
**Symptom**: Email is too formal for the creator's usual style, or too casual for a professional brand context.
**Fix**: Check the email intimacy level in the voice profile. A solopreneur coach should sound like a personal letter; a B2B software company should sound like a trusted advisor.

### 6. The "Generic Sign-Off" Problem
**Symptom**: Every email ends with "Best regards, [Name]" regardless of the creator's actual communication style.
**Fix**: Match the sign-off to the intimacy level and voice. It's a small thing that readers notice.
