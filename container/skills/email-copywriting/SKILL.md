# Email Copywriting Skill

Email is the most intimate content platform. Unlike social media (broadcast to thousands) or a blog post (permanent reference material), email is a letter. Your recipient reads it alone, usually on their phone, probably in the morning or evening. Write like you're writing to one person, because you are.

The best email marketers sound like a smart friend who also happens to be an expert in something you care about.

## Subject Line Framework

Subject lines are the most important copy you write. If nobody opens the email, nothing else matters.

### The Rules

- **40-60 characters** (ideal). Preview on mobile -- everything after ~50 chars gets cut.
- **No spam trigger words**: FREE (all caps), URGENT, guaranteed, winner, cash, click here, act now
- **No misleading subject lines**: The email must deliver on what the subject promises
- **No excessive punctuation**: One `!` per subject, maximum. Never `!!!`
- **Emoji**: Use sparingly and only if it fits the brand voice. One max, front or back.

### Subject Line Types (with examples)

**Curiosity** (create a knowledge gap):
- "The email mistake that costs coaches thousands"
- "What I learned after sending 500 cold emails"
- "Why your welcome sequence isn't working"
- Structure: State the outcome/problem without giving away the solution

**Benefit/Value** (clear promise):
- "5 subject line formulas that double open rates"
- "How to write a nurture sequence in one afternoon"
- "The re-engagement email that won back 40% of inactive subscribers"
- Structure: [Number or timeframe] + [specific outcome]

**Story/Personal** (hooks with humanity):
- "I almost quit email marketing in month 2"
- "The email I almost didn't send (it was my best)"
- "My first launch bombed. Here's what I did differently."
- Structure: First-person + vulnerable or surprising moment

**Number/List** (specific is credible):
- "3 things to do in your first email (most people skip #2)"
- "7 words to cut from your subject lines right now"
- "One-question survey (takes 30 seconds)"
- Structure: Specific number + clear scope

**Question** (engage directly):
- "Are you making this email mistake?"
- "What would you do with 20% better open rates?"
- "Is your email list actually working for you?"
- Structure: Rhetorical question that connects to their pain/desire

**Urgency** (real deadlines only):
- "Sale ends tonight at midnight"
- "Last call: [workshop name] closes Friday"
- "3 spots left (not a drill)"
- Structure: Real deadline + specific constraint
- WARNING: Use sparingly (max 1-2x per month). Overuse kills urgency.

**Re-engagement** (honest and direct):
- "Should I stop sending you emails?"
- "It's been a while. Still interested?"
- "One last thing before I let you go"
- Structure: Honest question + respectful tone. No guilt.

### Preview Text

Preview text is the grey text after the subject line in Gmail and most mobile clients. It's the second line of your pitch.

**Rules**:
- 90-140 characters ideal
- NEVER repeat the subject line (wasted space)
- Continue or complement the subject, don't explain it
- Can include a secondary hook or a specific detail

**Examples**:
- Subject: "The email mistake that costs coaches thousands"
- Preview: "And the 10-minute fix that changes your open rate permanently"

- Subject: "3 spots left (not a drill)"
- Preview: "The cohort starts Monday. I'd love to have you there."

## Email Body Structure

### The Core Pattern

Every email follows a version of this structure:

1. **Hook** (1-3 sentences): The first thing they read. Must answer "why am I reading this?"
2. **Context/Bridge** (2-4 sentences): Connect the hook to the value they're about to receive
3. **Value** (50-300 words): The meat -- the tip, story, insight, or proof
4. **CTA** (1-2 sentences + button/link): One clear next action

Optional:
- **P.S.** (1-2 sentences): Often the most read part of any email after the subject. Use for a secondary hook, a key reminder, or a personality moment.

### Formatting Rules (Mobile-First)

- **Paragraph length**: 1-3 sentences maximum. Long paragraphs disappear on mobile.
- **White space**: Generous. Dense email walls lose readers.
- **No centered text**: Left-aligned is more readable and personal
- **Bold sparingly**: Use to highlight a key phrase, not as decoration
- **Fonts**: One font family. 16px minimum body size.
- **Images**: Optional and usually not necessary. Pure text emails often outperform designed ones.

### Email Length Guidelines

| Email Type | Target Word Count | Why |
|-----------|------------------|-----|
| Welcome Email 1 (delivery) | 80-150 words | They want the thing, not an essay |
| Welcome Email 2-5 | 100-250 words | Value + relationship building |
| Nurture (value) | 150-350 words | Teach something meaningful |
| Nurture (story) | 200-400 words | Stories need room to breathe |
| Nurture (offer introduction) | 200-350 words | Clarity + conviction |
| Re-engagement | 80-150 words | Short = easier to act on |
| Promotional | 150-300 words | Enough to sell, not enough to bore |
| Newsletter | 300-600 words | Multiple sections justify more length |

## CTA (Call to Action) Principles

### One CTA Per Email

Every email should have exactly one primary CTA. Multiple competing CTAs reduce clicks on all of them.

### Outcome-Focused Language

Bad: "Click here"
Bad: "Learn more"
Bad: "Sign up"

Good: "Get the free guide"
Good: "Watch the 10-minute training"
Good: "See the full case study"
Good: "Reserve your spot"
Good: "Start your free trial"

The CTA should describe what they get, not what they do.

### CTA Button vs. Hyperlink

In automated sequences: Use a clear button (most platforms let you add buttons) for the primary CTA. Hyperlinks work fine for secondary or in-context links.

In personal-style newsletters: Plain text hyperlink can feel more authentic ("You can read it here" with "here" linked).

## Persuasion Frameworks

### PAS (Problem → Agitation → Solution)

Best for: Welcome sequences, promotional emails, re-engagement

1. **Problem**: Name the problem they have (specific, not generic)
2. **Agitation**: Dig into why this problem matters, what it costs them, why it won't solve itself
3. **Solution**: Introduce your solution as the way out

### AIDA (Attention → Interest → Desire → Action)

Best for: Campaign emails, product launches

1. **Attention**: Hook -- disrupt their pattern
2. **Interest**: Make them curious about what you're saying
3. **Desire**: Help them picture the outcome/result
4. **Action**: Ask them to take the next step

### Before-After-Bridge

Best for: Story-based nurture emails, case studies

1. **Before**: Paint the picture of where they are now (the problem state)
2. **After**: Paint the picture of where they could be (the desired state)
3. **Bridge**: Your solution/insight/method is how they get from before to after

### Feature → Advantage → Benefit (FAB)

Best for: Product emails where you're describing what you offer

1. **Feature**: What it is (a 12-week coaching program)
2. **Advantage**: Why it's better (you get direct feedback on your work, not just recorded lessons)
3. **Benefit**: What it means for them (you stop spinning your wheels and actually see results)

## Sequence-Specific Writing Guides

### Writing Welcome Sequences

**Email 1 (Day 0) -- The Delivery Email**:
- Lead with delivery. If they signed up for a guide, the guide link is in sentence 2.
- Set expectations: "Over the next 2 weeks, I'll also send you..."
- Keep it short. They want the thing, not your life story.
- Tone: Warm, excited, grateful they joined.

**Emails 2-4 -- Value Building**:
- Each email has ONE job: teach one thing, share one story, or give one resource.
- Don't try to cover everything. Specificity > comprehensiveness.
- Open with a specific hook -- reference something timely or a specific question their audience has.
- Close with a soft expectation for the next email: "Tomorrow I'll share..."

**Email 5-7 -- The Transition**:
- This is where you shift from "I'm here to help" to "here's how I specifically help."
- Don't pitch aggressively. Frame it as: "Here's how I work with people."
- Leave them with an easy next step, not a pressure close.

**The Welcome Sequence Voice Test**:
Read all 7 emails back-to-back. Do they sound like the same person wrote them? If email 1 is casual and warm but email 6 sounds like a sales deck, you have voice drift.

### Writing Nurture Sequences

**The Value Ladder**:
Plan the arc before writing. Map each email to one of these functions:
- Teach: Give away a specific insight, tactic, or framework
- Inspire: Show what's possible via story or case study
- Validate: Address doubts, objections, or fears
- Bridge: Connect their problem directly to your category of solution
- Offer: Introduce your specific offer clearly

**Avoiding the "tip spam" failure**:
Bad nurture sequence: 12 unrelated tips. Nobody sees the through-line.
Good nurture sequence: 12 emails with a clear arc, each one building on the last.

**The Value-First Rule**:
Emails 1-3 should give away so much value that the reader thinks "how is this free?" This is not waste -- it's the most effective way to build the trust required to sell.

### Writing Re-engagement Sequences

**The Honest Approach**:
The worst re-engagement emails pretend nothing is wrong. The best ones acknowledge the silence directly: "It's been a while. Life gets busy."

**What NOT to do**:
- "We've been so worried about you!" (manipulative)
- "DON'T LET US DOWN" (guilt)
- "FINAL WARNING: Your access is being revoked" (fake urgency)

**The Email 3 pattern** ("should I remove you?"):
"I want to keep sending you [specific value type]. But if email isn't working for you right now, that's completely fine. Just click here to stay, or ignore this and I'll remove you on [date]."

This email works because it treats the subscriber like an adult. It's also the highest-converting email in the sequence because it triggers a response from people who do want to stay.

### Writing Promotional / Campaign Emails

**The Honest Urgency Rule**:
Every deadline must be real. If your sale ends Friday, it ends Friday. If you extend it, you're training your list to ignore all future urgency permanently.

**When you must create scarcity**:
- Cohort limits (you can only take 10 clients) -- if true, state it
- Bonus deadlines (the bonus goes away at midnight) -- announce it early, honor it
- Early bird pricing (price goes up after [date]) -- common and accepted if genuine

**Campaign email arc**:
- Email 1: Lead with story, context, or excitement. The "why this now" email. Low pressure.
- Email 2: Social proof. Testimonials, results, case studies. Let others sell for you.
- Email 3: Objection handling. The FAQ email. What are they worried about? Answer it directly.
- Email 4: Urgency reminder. Not "BUY NOW" but "Heads up -- [deadline] is approaching."
- Email 5: Last chance. Direct, clear, no apology. "This is the last email about [offer]."

### Writing Newsletter Templates

**The One-Page Principle**:
A newsletter template should be completable in under 2 hours. If it takes 5 hours to fill in, they won't do it consistently. Consistency beats perfection for newsletters.

**Template philosophy**:
Give them the structure, write the instructions, and provide an example in their voice. The example shows them what "done" looks like. Without an example, a blank template is just intimidating.

**The hook section**:
Every newsletter needs a personal opening hook -- something they're thinking about, something that happened this week, something they learned. This is what separates a newsletter from a blast. Keep it 2-4 sentences.

## Subject Line Quality Checklist

Before approving a subject line, ask:
1. Is it 40-60 characters?
2. Does the email actually deliver on what this subject line promises?
3. Would a spam filter flag this?
4. Is this specific to this business/audience, or could it work for any business?
5. Does it match the brand's voice? (Would they actually write this?)

## Common Copywriting Failures

| Failure | Example | Fix |
|---------|---------|-----|
| Weak subject line | "Newsletter #47" | "The cold email mistake I made for 2 years" |
| Vague CTA | "Click here to learn more" | "Get the 5-step checklist" |
| Too long | 800-word nurture email | 200-300 words, link to long-form for those who want more |
| Corporate tone drift | "We are pleased to inform you that..." | "Good news -- here's what happened" |
| No preview text | (blank preview) | Write a complementary hook |
| Repeated subject + preview | Subject: "New blog post" / Preview: "New blog post now live" | Preview: "Why we stopped using the strategy everyone recommends" |
| Generic value prop | "Our product helps you grow your business" | "Our tool saved our first 100 customers an average of 6 hours/week" |
| Fake urgency | "Act now before it's too late!" | "Sale ends Sunday at midnight. No extensions." |
