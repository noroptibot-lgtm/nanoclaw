# Email Outreach Skill

This skill provides the methodology for writing outreach that opens doors by using email marketing deliverables as proof of work. The key insight: most businesses have an email list they're not using. The list is a sleeping asset. Showing them what their subscribers should be receiving is more powerful than telling them you could build it.

## The "Sleeping Asset" Sales Motion

The core pitch:
> "Your email list has [X] subscribers who signed up because they wanted to hear from you. Here's what they should be receiving."

This works because:
1. It's specific -- you're referencing their actual business, their actual list
2. It creates immediate, tangible loss aversion -- they can see exactly what they're missing
3. The welcome sequence sample *shows* the capability rather than describing it
4. It's not aggressive -- you're giving them something, not pressuring them

**If list size is known**: "Your list of 2,300 subscribers hasn't heard from you in 4 months. Here's the welcome sequence they should have received when they signed up."

**If list size is unknown**: "Your subscribers signed up because they wanted to hear from you. Here's what they're missing."

**If no list exists yet**: "You have a signup form on your website but no automation behind it. Here's what a visitor who opts in should receive." (Less powerful but still relevant)

## Core Outreach Pattern

Every outreach piece follows the same pattern:
1. **I noticed something specific about your email setup** (shows research)
2. **There's a gap -- your list is a sleeping asset** (creates urgency)
3. **I went ahead and built something** (proves capability)
4. **Here's what it looks like** (concrete deliverables)
5. **Want to talk about getting it live?** (soft CTA)

## Cover Letter / Cold Email Framework

### The Structure (150-250 words)

**Subject Lines** (3 options per email):
1. **Proof hook**: References what you built ("I built an email system for [Company]")
2. **Gap hook**: References the sleeping asset ("Your email list has [X] subscribers who haven't heard from you")
3. **Curiosity hook**: Creates intrigue ("Something I noticed about [Company]'s email setup")

**Email Body**:

**Opening (1-2 sentences)**:
- Reference their specific email presence (or absence) -- show you looked at their site
- Mention something specific: the signup form on their homepage, the newsletter that hasn't been updated since [month], the lead magnet they offer with no sequence behind it
- NO flattery filler ("I'm a huge fan of your work", "I was so impressed")
- NO generic opener ("I hope this email finds you well")

**The Gap (2-3 sentences)**:
- The sleeping asset framing -- what they have vs. what they're missing
- Frame as opportunity, not criticism: "Your business has an audience that's ready to hear from you"
- Be specific to this prospect: don't write something that could apply to any business

**What I Built (1-2 sentences)**:
- "I went ahead and built it."
- **Full mode**: Reference the complete email system with specific deliverables
- **Light mode**: Reference the email audit + welcome sequence sample (3 emails)
- Specificity is everything here -- "I wrote your first 3 emails" beats "I built an email sequence"

**The CTA (1-2 sentences)**:
- One soft ask: suggest a short call to walk through it
- Include member's name and email
- No pricing. No pressure. No fake urgency.

### Writing Rules for Cover Letters

- **First person** from the member's perspective ("I noticed", "I built", "I took a look")
- **Conversational, not corporate** -- match the member's niche tone
- **Specific, not generic** -- reference THIS prospect's actual email presence from the business brief
- **No pricing** -- pricing comes in conversation
- **No flattery filler** -- "hope this finds you well", "I'm a huge fan", etc.
- **150-250 words** -- short enough to actually get read
- **Single gap focus** -- the sleeping asset is the gap
- **3 subject lines** -- always provide all 3 (proof, gap, curiosity)
- **Standalone file** -- the email is complete on its own; attachments added separately

### Light Mode vs. Full Mode Email Differences

**Light mode cold email**:
- Say "I took a look at your email setup" NOT "I built a complete email system"
- Reference the 2 deliverables: email audit snapshot + welcome sequence sample (3 emails)
- CTA: "Want to see the full system -- nurture flows, campaigns, newsletter templates, the works?"
- Do NOT mention sequences beyond the welcome sample, platform setup guide, segmentation strategy, etc.

**Full mode cover letter**:
- "I went ahead and built a complete email marketing system for [Company]"
- Reference the full package: welcome sequence, nurture flow, re-engagement sequence, 3 campaign frameworks, newsletter template, subject line swipe file, segmentation strategy, and platform setup guide
- CTA: "Want to walk through the system and talk about getting it live?"

### Example Subject Lines (adapt to specific business)

**Proof hooks**:
- "I built an email system for [Company]"
- "I wrote [Company]'s first 7 emails"
- "Your email system -- built and ready to review"

**Gap hooks**:
- "Your email list has [X] subscribers who haven't heard from you"
- "The [Company] welcome sequence your subscribers never received"
- "Your list is a sleeping asset"

**Curiosity hooks**:
- "Something I noticed about [Company]'s email setup"
- "The [Company] subscribers who opted in and heard nothing"
- "What your email signup form isn't doing"

## Revenue Framing (for the Gap section)

When crafting the gap paragraph, you can optionally use revenue math to make the sleeping asset concrete:

**If list size is known**:
> "At an industry average of $1/subscriber/month, your list of [X] subscribers represents $[X×1]/month in revenue that's not being captured."

**General framing** (if list size is unknown):
> "A well-built email system typically generates $1-5 per subscriber per month in additional revenue. That's a list of untapped potential sitting in your email platform right now."

**E-commerce framing**:
> "A welcome sequence alone typically converts 5-15% of new subscribers into first-time buyers. For a business with consistent signups, that's [estimated number] sales you're currently leaving on the table."

Use revenue framing selectively -- it adds power to the gap paragraph but can feel manipulative if overused. Use it when the numbers are compelling and genuine.

## Quality Standards for Outreach

Every cold email should be:

1. **Specific**: References the recipient's actual business, not a template
2. **Value-first**: Gives something (the deliverables) before asking
3. **Concise**: Respects their time (150-250 words)
4. **No pressure**: Soft CTA, no urgency manufacturing
5. **Professional**: No typos, no over-familiarity
6. **Credible**: The deliverables themselves are the proof; no need to claim credentials
7. **Actionable**: Clear, specific next step (a call, not just "reach out")

## Red Flags (Fail Patterns)

| Red Flag | Why It Fails |
|---------|-------------|
| "I hope this email finds you well" | Signals a template -- no human starts emails this way |
| "I'm a huge fan of your work" | Flattery + can't verify = not credible |
| Pricing in the cold email | Closes doors before they're open |
| "URGENT" or aggressive language | Screams spam, breaks trust |
| Over 300 words | Nobody reads a 300-word cold email |
| Generic claims ("I help businesses like yours") | Could apply to anyone -- adds zero value |
| Multiple CTAs | Diffuses attention -- one ask only |
| No mention of what you built | The deliverable is the point -- lead with it |
