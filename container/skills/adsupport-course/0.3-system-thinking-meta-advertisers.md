Part 1 - The Machine Behind the CurtainHow Meta’s algorithm “thinks” and why advertisers fail when they fight it
Most people new to running ads treat Meta like a digital billboard.
They think, “I’ll just target my ideal customer and show them an ad.”
But Meta isn’t a billboard.
It’s a machine-learning brain trained on trillions of data points, every tap, scroll, comment, purchase, and hesitation.
Its real job isn’t to show your ad to your target audience.
Its job is to maximise total user engagement across the platform.
Think of it like a stock market of attention:
Billions of “micro auctions” happen every second.
Each user’s attention is a tradable asset.
Meta sells that attention to advertisers… but only if the trade benefits the user experience too.
If your ad makes people stop scrolling, engage, and even book a call or buy, you’re rewarded with cheaper CPMs and more impressions.
If your ad annoys people, you get priced out of the market.

2. How the Algorithm “Sees” the World
Meta doesn’t see people, it sees patterns of behavior.
Every user is represented as a cluster of probabilities:
“This user has a 0.7 probability of watching a fitness VSL, a 0.3 probability of clicking a call-to-action, and a 0.05 probability of submitting a lead form.”
Your job as an advertiser is to feed the algorithm clear, consistent data so it can find more people like the ones who convert.
But here’s where most people new to ads go wrong, they assume they know who their ideal buyer is.
You might say:
“I help agency owners doing $10k/month scale to $50k/month.”
Meta translates that into:
“Okay, let’s find people who behave like those who already watched your VSL and booked a call.”
In other words, Meta doesn’t target demographics; it targets behavior.
That’s why broad targeting often outperforms hyper-specific interests: the algorithm already knows who’s in-market before you do.
3. The Feedback Loop That Runs Everything
Meta’s delivery system is a massive feedback loop of three moving parts:
Your Input Signals - the data you feed it (ads, audiences, conversion events).
User Response Data - how people engage with your ads (watch time, CTR, conversions, feedback).
Model Adjustments - how Meta recalibrates its predictions for who to show your ad to next.
Every impression is a test.
Every click and conversion updates the model.
Think of it like training a hunting dog:
You point it in the general direction (your audience).
You reward it when it finds the right target (your conversions).
Over time, it gets smarter, if you keep giving it consistent signals.
If you break the loop by resetting learning, swapping pixels, or killing ad sets too early and it forgets what it learned.
4. Why Advertisers Fail: Fighting the Machine
The biggest mistake new advertisers make is trying to control or hack the algorithm instead of coach it. Any hack or novel campaign structure might work for now but it won’t win over the long-haul.
They over-segment audiences, duplicate ad sets, and micromanage budgets which splits data across more adsets and prevents the system from finding signal density.
Remember:
The algorithm isn’t stupid. It just needs clarity, consistency, and volume.
If you confuse it, you lose efficiency.
If you feed it good data, it compounds learning exponentially.
5. What You Can Do to “Work With” the System
To win on Meta, your mindset has to shift from “targeting” to “training.”
Here’s how:
Principle

Simplify structure
	Use 1–3 ad sets per campaign, not 10.
	Allows Meta to find the best pockets of buyers faster.

Feed it consistent quality data
	Don’t switch objectives, events, or domains.
	Keeps the conversion model stable.

Let it learn
	Give ad sets at least 50 conversions before judging.
	You’re teaching the system what success looks like.

Optimise for quality signals
	Use high-retention ads, strong landing pages, and proper event setup.
	Improves predictive accuracy of Meta’s models.
Part 2 - Inside the AuctionHow Meta decides which ads to show, what you actually pay for, and how to influence the outcome.
1. What the Auction Really Is
Every time a user opens Facebook or Instagram, thousands of advertisers are competing for that single impression.
But unlike a traditional auction where the highest bidder wins, Meta’s auction rewards the best user experience.
In other words:
The winner isn’t the ad with the biggest budget/bid - it’s the ad that is deemed most valuable to both Meta and the user.
That “value” is calculated in milliseconds through a formula that determines Total Value for every eligible ad.
2. The Formula That Runs It All
Meta publicly defines the Total Value formula as:
Total Value = Bid × Estimated Action Rate + Ad Quality
Let’s break that down.
Variable
	What It Means
	What You Can Control

Bid
	How much you’re willing to pay for your optimisation event (via your bid strategy).
	Budget + bid strategy choice (lowest cost, cost cap, etc.)

Estimated Action Rate (EAR)
	How likely a user is to perform the action you’re optimising for.
	Your targeting, offer, and creative relevance.

Ad Quality
	How positive or negative users’ interactions with your ad have been (feedback, watch time, hides, etc.)
	Your creative quality, messaging accuracy, and post-click experience.

So, while your bid matters, EAR and Ad Quality often matter more.
That’s why great ads can outcompete lazy ones even with lower budgets.
3. The Bid: How You Enter the Race
When you set up a campaign, you’re choosing how you want Meta to bid for impressions.
There are a few main bid strategies in 2025:
Strategy
	Description
	Use When

Lowest Cost (Auto Bid)
	Meta spends your budget to get the most results for the lowest cost.
	Most campaigns, especially during learning or testing.

Cost Cap
	You tell Meta your target CPA, and it bids dynamically to hit that average.
	Scaling when you already have stable data and consistent conversion volume.

Bid Cap
	You set a hard ceiling on bids.
	Rarely used; only for ultra-tight control or retargeting.
👉 For most info-product advertisers, “Lowest Cost” performs best until you have at least 50+ conversions and a proven funnel.
4. Estimated Action Rate: Meta’s Crystal Ball
EAR is Meta’s prediction of how likely a given user is to take your desired action.It’s based on:
Past behavior of similar users
Historical engagement with your ads
Conversion data from your pixel or CAPI
In practice, that means:
If your ad consistently gets people to watch your ad for 30+ seconds, your EAR improves.
If your landing page converts well and you’ve integrated your pixel properly, EAR rises.
The algorithm learns who converts, how they behave, and what they respond to.
Then it starts finding lookalike users automatically, even inside “broad” targeting.
So EAR is a performance multiplier: when it rises, CPMs drop, CTRs rise, and the system finds easier wins.
5. Ad Quality: The Invisible Multiplier
Ad quality is Meta’s hidden filter, it protects the user experience.
It’s determined by:
Engagement Quality: Are people watching, clicking, and commenting?
Negative Feedback: Are people hiding your ad, marking it as irrelevant, or bouncing too quickly?
Relevance & Clarity: Does your ad copy align with what users see after they click?
Even small issues like misleading hooks, broken promises, or poor mobile experience can drop your ad quality score and increase your CPM by 30–50%.
Think of Ad Quality as your “reputation score” with Meta.
Great ads get a “discount.” Bad ads pay a “tax.”
6. How Ads Are Ranked (Step-by-Step)
Here’s what happens in each micro-auction (simplified):
User Opens App: Meta loads eligible ads that could be shown to that person.
Filters Apply: Ads are filtered based on audience eligibility, placement, and ad set delivery settings.
Total Value Calculated: Each ad’s total value (Bid × EAR + Quality) is calculated.
Winner Chosen: The highest total value ad wins and gets delivered.
Second Price Rule Applied: You pay just above what the second-highest total value would have required.
You don’t pay your bid, you pay the minimum needed to beat the next-best ad.

7. What This Means for You as an Advertiser
If you understand the Total Value formula, you understand how to influence delivery without increasing the budget.
Here’s how each variable connects to real-world levers:
Variable
	What Improves It
	Example

Bid
	Budget or bid strategy
	Raise budget on proven ad sets. Use cost caps when scaling.

EAR
	Ad relevance, pixel accuracy, optimisation event
	Clean data via CAPI + Pixel, optimise for purchase/booked call, not link clicks.

Ad Quality
	Hook clarity, ethical claims, user experience
	Ensure your VSL matches ad promise; use compliant messaging.

8. The Advertiser’s Mindset
The best advertisers don’t try to hack the system, they play the game Meta wants them to play and just do it better than others.
They design their campaigns so that every impression delivers high predicted value to the system.
That’s why they:
Use one clear CTA per ad.
Keep funnel messaging consistent (ad → landing → VSL → offer).
Test creative angles, not tiny tweaks or micro-targeting.
When your ad delivers what it promises and also drives engagement, you climb the internal “trust ladder.”Meta rewards that with more reach and lower costs.
Part 3 - The Learning Phase and Delivery SystemHow Meta’s system actually learns, why campaigns behave erratically at first, and how to stabilise performance.
1. What the Learning Phase Actually Is
When you launch a campaign, Meta’s algorithm doesn’t know who will convert yet.It starts with your inputs e.g. your audience, optimisation event, creative, and landing page, and begins testing.
This early stage is called the ‘Learning Phase’.
Meta describes it as:“A period when the delivery system is exploring the best way to deliver your ads based on available data.”
Think of it as a calibration period.Meta is observing who engages, how they convert, and what patterns predict success.
Every impression, click, and conversion feeds this model until it’s confident enough to predict who your best buyers are.
Imagine you’ve just hired a new salesperson.For the first few weeks, they’re figuring out which leads are hot, which objections matter, and how to pitch effectively.The learning phase is that salesperson’s “onboarding.”
If you fire them (i.e. reset learning) before they finish learning, you start from zero again.
2. When You’re in Learning, and When You’re Out
Meta considers an ad set to be “in learning” until it achieves about 50 optimisation events within a 7-day window (e.g., 50 leads, purchases, or booked calls).
Once it passes that threshold, the model stabilises and enters the Learning Limited or Stable phase.
Let’s define the states:
Status
	What It Means
	What to Do

Learning
	The system is exploring who converts. Performance is volatile.
	Avoid major edits. Let data accumulate.

Learning Limited
	Not enough conversions to exit learning.
	Simplify structure, combine ad sets, or broaden targeting.

Active (Stable)
	The model has enough data to optimise efficiently.
	Now you can scale or duplicate.
3. What Resets the Learning Phase
The most common mistake advertisers make is resetting learning unintentionally.
Any significant edit to an ad set tells Meta:
“Forget what you knew - start over.”
Here’s what triggers a reset:
Action
	Effect

Changing audience targeting
	Resets learning

Changing optimisation event (e.g., from leads to purchases)
	Resets learning

Making large budget changes (±20%+ at once)
	Resets learning

Editing ad creative (headline, copy, video)
	Resets learning

Duplicating ad sets or ads
	Creates a new learning phase
Each time you reset learning, the algorithm must rebuild its predictive model, costing you time and efficiency.
Conspiracy Theory:There is a school of thinking that Meta makes the majority of their return due to the inefficiency created by the learning phase so exiting is important and should be achieved when budget/performance allows.
4. How the Delivery System Learns Over Time
Once an ad set exits learning, the algorithm continuously refines who sees your ads based on conversion feedback.
It’s a three-layer optimisation process:
User Level: Who is most likely to take the desired action.
Placement Level: Where those people are most responsive (Feed, Reels, Stories, etc.).
Time Level: When they’re most active or likely to convert.
This is why you don’t need to manually pick placements or run 10 ad sets, the system self-optimises if you give it freedom and enough data.
5. What “Learning Limited” Really Means
When you see “Learning Limited” in Ads Manager, it doesn’t mean the campaign is failing,  it just means Meta hasn’t seen enough conversions to stabilise.
For info-product offers, this often happens because:
You’re optimising for high-value events (like “Book a Call”) that don’t happen frequently enough.
Budgets are too small to hit 50 events/week.
Too many ad sets split your spend and data.
You can fix it by: ✅ Consolidating ad sets (1–2 per campaign). ✅ Increasing budget slightly. ✅ Optimising for a higher-volume upstream event (like “Lead”).
Remember: the system needs volume before precision.
6. The Role of Conversion Events and the Pixel
Your optimisation event is Meta’s “north star.”
It tells the algorithm: this is what success looks like.
For example, if you optimise for Book a Call, the algorithm finds people likely to book calls, not just click links.
That’s why event setup (via Pixel and/or Conversion API) matters:
If  your conversion data is missing, delayed, duplicated or inaccurate, Meta can’t learn correctly.
7. The Real Goal: Signal Density
The learning phase isn’t about time - it’s about signal density.
Meta doesn’t care how long your campaign runs.It cares how much clean, consistent conversion data it can gather.
The faster you can feed it 50+ high-quality conversions (same event, same domain, same pixel), the faster it stabilises.
Think of data as oxygen for the algorithm.Without enough oxygen, performance suffocates.
8. How to “Train” the Algorithm Intelligently
Here’s how to feed the system efficiently:
Principle
	What It Means in Practice

Start simple
	One campaign, 2-3 ad sets, one conversion event.

Feed the same event consistently
	Don’t switch from calls → purchases mid-test.

Give it time and volume
	Aim for 50+ events per week.

Avoid early edits
	Let ads run at least 3–5 days before optimisation.

Monitor frequency and CTR
	If CTR > 1.5% and CVR is steady, don’t touch it.

Part 4 - Audiences: Warm, Interests, Lookalike & Advantage+How Meta finds your buyers and why “broad” often outperforms hyper-targeting
1. The Big Shift: From Targeting to Prediction
Five years ago, targeting on Facebook meant manually choosing interests, demographics, and behaviors.
You’d build a detailed avatar, “men aged 25–35, interested in online business and ClickFunnels”, and hope it matched your market.
You might have a 3-phase system and test 10 audiences with a $5 micro-budget.
But by 2025, that model/strategy is largely obsolete.
Today, Meta doesn’t need you to tell it who your buyer is.It has trillions of behavioral signals from across the app ecosystem (Facebook, Instagram, Messenger, WhatsApp, and partner sites).
Meta no longer targets people based on what they say they like - it targets people based on what they do.
That’s why your audience’s definition is now less about inputs (interests, age, gender) and more about outputs (what actions your buyers take).
2. The Four Main Audience Types in 2025
Let’s break down each audience category and how Meta actually treats them behind the scenes:
Type
	Description
	Best Use Case

Core Audiences
	Manual targeting using interests, age, gender, location, etc.
	Local businesses, hyper-niche markets, or when no pixel data exists yet.

Custom Audiences
	Built from your first-party data (website visitors, leads, customers, etc.).
	Retargeting and warm audiences.

Lookalike Audiences
	Modeled from a source Custom Audience: finds people who “look like” your converters.
	Cold acquisition when you have clean conversion data.

Advantage+ Audiences
	Meta’s new “smart broad” system - automatically expands beyond your set parameters to find likely converters.
	The new default for most advertisers (including for info-products).
3. Core Audiences: Manual Targeting’s Decline
Targeting still exists, but its influence has diminished.
When you select interests like Entrepreneurship, Online Business, Marketing, Meta uses them as light suggestions, not hard filters.
The system will still prioritise predicted converters over interest matches, meaning even inside a narrow interest, delivery will drift toward people most likely to convert, regardless of your demographic setup.
4. Custom Audiences: Your Retargeting Foundation
Custom audiences are built from your first-party data, which means you’re feeding Meta real behavior.
The main types include:
Website visitors (via Pixel or CAPI)
Video viewers (e.g., 25%, 50%, 75% watch time)
Leads
Customer lists (emails, phone numbers)
Engaged users (Instagram profile visits, post interactions, etc.)
Meta treats these audiences as behavioral fingerprints.Every person in these segments gives the algorithm context: “This is the kind of person we want more of.”
That’s why strong retargeting and event data supercharge your cold acquisition campaigns too, the algorithm uses these behavioral patterns to inform future prospecting.

Each feeding the next via behavioral data.
5. Lookalike Audiences: Meta’s First Generation of AI Modeling
A Lookalike Audience is Meta’s first predictive system, it finds new people who behave like your source audience.
If your source audience (Custom Audience) is small or low-quality, your Lookalike will be too.If it’s clean and conversion-rich, it becomes useful.
Lookalike audiences are segmented by country.
Best practices in 2025:
Use high-quality source data (ideally 500–1,000+ purchasers, leads, or call-bookers, minimum is 100 in a single country).
Choose the right seed event (don’t use “All Website Visitors”, use Qualified calls or Clients
Start with 1%–2% size for precision; expand later for volume/scale.
However, and this is key, lookalikes are being slowly replaced by Advantage+ audiences, which perform similar modeling automatically.
6. Advantage+ Audiences: The 2025 Default?
When you enable Advantage+ Audience Expansion, you’re telling Meta:
“Ignore my audience filters if you think someone outside them will convert.”
In practice, that means Meta starts broad but constantly refines targeting based on conversion behavior, creative response, and account-level learning.
Think of Advantage+ as Meta’s “open world mode.”You give it the goal, budget, and creative, and it tries hunts down the best buyers, even in unexpected places.
For info-product funnels, this usually outperforms manual interest targeting at scale because:
It uses aggregated learning from your entire ad account.
It adapts to creative performance and funnel feedback in real time.
It leverages Meta’s cross-platform behavioral prediction model (Facebook, Instagram, Reels).
However in the beginning while we are building pixel data and at a lower budget, being more specific with your targeting is an advantage
7. How Meta Combines Audience Signals
Regardless of which audience type you use, Meta doesn’t treat them in isolation.It blends signals from three key categories:
Advertiser-defined signals - your targeting, creative, conversion event.
User behavior signals - scroll patterns, engagement, watch time, and conversion likelihood.
System learning signals - aggregated insights from millions of other advertisers and users.
The delivery system merges these to predict which users will produce the most total value (remember the auction formula from Part 2).
That’s why two advertisers can target the exact same audience but get completely different results, because Meta’s system weighs their creative, data quality, and conversion signals differently.

Imagine your audiences like different fishing nets.
Advantage+ is a wide trawling net that uses sonar to find fish you didn’t know existed.

The trick is letting the system use all three intelligently instead of overfishing one small pond.
Part 5 - Creative, Conversion & Feedback SignalsHow Meta reads your ads, and how to train the algorithm to send you better traffic.
1. The Invisible Scoring System
Every single ad you launch on Meta gets an invisible “scorecard.”
This score isn’t something you see in Ads Manager, but it determines everything:
How often your ad is shown
How much you pay per impression
Whether Meta trusts your ad enough to expand delivery
That score is driven by signals.Think of these signals like Meta’s “language” and the way it interprets what your ad is doing, how people react, and whether showing it more is good or bad for the user experience.
2. The Three Signal Pillars
Meta’s creative evaluation system can be broken down into three primary signal pillars:
Pillar
	Description
	Examples of What Meta Tracks

Engagement Signals
	How users interact with your ad.
	Likes, comments, shares, saves, watch time, scroll speed, click-through rate (CTR).

Conversion Signals
	What happens after they click or view.
	Page view rate, form submissions, lead conversions, call bookings, purchases.

Feedback Signals
	How users feel about your ad.
	Negative feedback (hide ad, report, “not relevant”), survey quality scores, ad fatigue metrics.
All three feed into the Total Value formula from Part 2.So your cost isn’t just based on bids, it’s based on whether your ad adds value or irritates the user base.
3. Engagement: The “First Impression” Metric
When an ad first launches, Meta doesn’t yet know if it will convert.So it watches front-end engagement like a hawk.
Scroll speed: Do people pause or skip?
Watch time: How long do they watch your video before scrolling away?
CTR: Do they click to learn more or ignore it?
Early engagement signals tell Meta:“This ad might be worth testing with more people.”
That’s why short-form, pattern-interrupt creative styles dominate the early phase, they buy you time and attention so Meta can gather conversion data.
But beware: clickbait engagement without downstream conversions leads to punishment later.Meta’s system notices if people click but don’t complete your conversion event, and your costs will rise accordingly.
4. Conversion: The “Proof” Metric
Once enough engagement data is gathered, Meta starts looking for proof that the ad drives real outcomes.
This is where your pixel and Conversion API (CAPI) setup becomes critical.
Meta can only optimise toward what it sees.
If you don’t pass back accurate conversion events (lead, booked call, purchase), Meta’s learning loops break.
The conversion system relies on event prioritisation. In 2025, Meta’s Event Match Quality (EMQ) score and Aggregated Event Measurement (AEM) still determine how much data your pixel sends back, the cleaner and more consistent your event tracking, the smarter your delivery becomes.
Common mistakes:
Optimising for “Link Clicks” instead of “Book a Call” (wrong signal entirely).
Using weak lead forms (Meta counts them, but they produce low-quality signals).
Not using CAPI  (meaning half your conversions aren’t reported after iOS privacy updates.)
Always optimise for the highest-intent event your funnel can track with reasonable volume (e.g. “Schedule”, not “Landing Page Views”).
Even if you get fewer conversions initially, the algorithm learns the quality of your buyers, not just quantity.
5. Feedback: The Silent Killer
Meta’s algorithm fiercely protects user experience.If people hide your ad, report it, or skip quickly, the system throttles delivery and spikes your CPM, even if your CTR looks strong.
Feedback signals include:
Negative reactions (Hide Ad, Report Ad, “Not relevant”)
Ad frequency fatigue (same user sees ad too often)
Post-click experience (slow landing pages, deceptive claims)
Think of feedback as “trust points.”Lose too many, and you’re no longer invited to the premium auction pool, your CPMs skyrocket and delivery slows to a crawl.
Practical ways to maintain high feedback scores:
Refresh creatives every 7–14 days.
Use social proof and empathy, not hype or fake scarcity.
Make sure landing pages load fast and align with the ad’s promise.
Avoid “this will change your life” type claims (triggers ad disapproval and negative sentiment).
6. The Feedback Loop: How Meta Learns Over Time
Every impression generates a small feedback packet:
Did they pause?
Did they click?
Did they convert?
Did they react negatively?
Those micro-events feed into your ad set’s performance model, and the algorithm constantly updates its predictions:
“People like this one → show it more.”“People bounce instantly → show it less.”
This loop runs millions of times per day per ad account.And because it’s cross-referenced with billions of other advertisers, your ad’s performance is always relative to what else people could be seeing in that same slot.
That’s why your cost per lead can fluctuate even if your ad doesn’t change, the competition and system sentiment around your creative do.
7. What “Good” Creative Signals Look Like
Here’s a simple rule: Meta rewards ads that create good experiences.

	Content relevance
	50%+ average

If you maintain positive engagement and clean conversion signals, Meta labels your ad as “high quality,” which drops your CPMs and increases your reach in the auction.
8. Practical Levers to “Train” the Algorithm
Every high-performing advertiser does the same few things differently:
Use strong conversion signals early.Go straight to the lead or schedule event.
Keep creatives fresh.Every 7–14 days, test new angles or hooks to reset engagement metrics.
Let winners run long enough.Don’t kill ads in the first 48 hours, let the learning phase gather enough signals (50 conversions per week minimum).
Avoid bait-and-switch messaging.Meta’s feedback systems now analyse landing page text too. Misaligned claims hurt delivery.
Test creative diversity, not audience micromanagement.One ad set, multiple creatives, same audience → the system self-selects winners faster.

10. Key Takeaways
Meta ranks ads by signal quality, not just bids.
Early engagement gets you seen; sustained conversion keeps you scaling.
Feedback signals (negative reactions, ad fatigue) quietly raise costs over time.
Creative diversity and event accuracy are your biggest performance levers.
The algorithm is always learning from you, make sure it’s learning the right things.
[Data In] → [Auction Prediction] → [Learning Optimisation] → [Delivery Feedback] → [Data Out → Improved Prediction]
